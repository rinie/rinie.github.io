Steve Wozniak appeared on *The Big Bang Theory*. Penny recognised him immediately. The physicists did not.

Not because they were unaware of Wozniak. They knew exactly who he was — in the abstract, in the domain, as a name in the history of computing. But in the room, in the moment, as a person standing in front of them, they missed him. They were looking at the wrong layer.

Penny was looking at the person.

---

## Three Feedback Loops

There is a recurring pattern in how intelligent systems fail to hear the signals around them. Three characters from two different fictions illustrate it more precisely than any framework.

**Zaphod Beeblebrox** — feedback loop absent because of ego. Every signal that arrives is processed as confirmation. He survived the Total Perspective Vortex — the device that shows you your true insignificance in the cosmos and drives everyone else mad — because the universe it showed him was a simulation built specifically for him, in which he was the most important thing. The feedback was technically delivered. The signal was false. He walked out confirmed rather than corrected.

This is the vanity metric at cosmological scale. The measurement arrived. The instrument worked correctly. The data was designed to produce the answer the Def expected. Reality was never actually accessed.

**Sheldon Cooper** — feedback loop absent because of architecture. The social signal parser is genuinely not there. Sarcasm requires a theory of mind — you have to model that the speaker means the opposite of what they said. Sheldon does not have that parser for social signals. "That was sarcasm" has to be announced. Even then he files it as a data point rather than feeling it.

Not the same failure as Zaphod. Zaphod could hear the signal and chooses not to. Sheldon cannot hear it and would listen if he could.

**Steve Wozniak** — correctly calibrated loop. Low threshold. Signal arrives, lands proportionally, produces response proportional to its significance. Not anxious. Not defensive. Just listening.

When Penny recognised him she was genuinely pleased to meet him. He was genuinely pleased that someone recognised him. No performance. No ego. Just two people in a room, one of whom had built something and was delighted it was remembered. The feedback arrived. It landed. Mostly harmless.

---

## Penny Is the Thread

Penny routes around Sheldon's missing parser. She stops using sarcasm with him, not because sarcasm was wrong but because the channel does not exist. The interface adapts to the receiver.

Penny spotted Woz while the physicists were busy in their own domain expertise. Not because she knew more about computing — she did not. Because she was looking at the person in front of her rather than at the abstract category "visiting guest."

The 90% signal is always in the room. The experts are often looking at the wrong layer to see it.

The GPP failure is the system that insists the user should adapt to the interface. The cookie banner that makes rejection impossible. The chatbot that says "I'm happy to help!" before giving a wrong answer. The Vortex that shows you a universe calibrated to your ego. All of them insisting the signal should arrive in the format the Def expects, rather than adapting to receive the signal in the format it actually comes.

## Penny and the Ikea Closet

Penny needed to assemble an Ikea closet. The guys offered to help — and immediately began analysing the structural deficiencies of the design, the suboptimal dowel joints, the reinforcement strategies they would apply to produce something superior to what Ikea had shipped.

Penny assembled it herself. With a hex torque.

Not a hex key — a hex torque. She named the tool slightly wrong. She used it exactly right. She looked at the physical object in front of her, found the thing that turned the bolts, and assembled the closet. The closet stood up. The clothes had somewhere to go.

The guys' MIT approach was not wrong about the engineering. It was wrong about the task. The task was: assemble the closet. The Def-Push approach analysed the problem. The Use-Pull approach solved it.

Penny went to the Gemba. She looked at the actual parts. The physical object told her what to do next. She routed around the instructions at every point where they failed her — not by ignoring them but by looking at the thing rather than the diagram of the thing.

The hex torque is the external resolver. She did not know the tool's correct name. She knew its function. The Semantic intent (turn this bolt) resolved to the correct Gutenberg implementation (this tool) without passing through the official catalogue. The resolver worked. The closet stands.

## Trillian Packed for the End of the World

Trillian is not the smartest person on the Heart of Gold by any credential the universe recognises. Zaphod has two heads. Ford has been a researcher for fifteen years. Marvin has a brain the size of a planet.

Trillian is the one who knows where the towel is.

She packed for the end of the world. She understood the Gutenberg reality — we are leaving Earth permanently, bring what matters — while everyone else was still processing the Semantic shock that the Earth was gone. She had the mice. She knew the coordinates. She could actually fly the ship when Zaphod was being Zaphod.

Trillian and Penny are the same character. Both underestimated by the men around them who have more credentials and less practical competence. Both have correctly calibrated feedback loops. Both go to the Gemba. Both find the hex torque. Both know where the towel is.

The crew of the Heart of Gold and the guys on The Big Bang Theory share the same failure: brilliant at the domain they studied, helpless at the domain in front of them. Sheldon can derive string theory and cannot parse sarcasm. Zaphod can steal the most improbable ship in the universe and cannot understand why Marvin is depressed. The expertise is real. The practical calibration is missing.

## Life Comes Without a Manual

Life is the flat-pack. No manual. Just the sketchy Ikea drawings — some intuition, some pattern recognition from watching other people assemble theirs, some tribal knowledge passed down in ways nobody explicitly planned.

The sketchy drawing is the MVP. Not because it is a good drawing. Because it is enough to start. Enough to find the next turn. The complete specification would take longer to produce than the assembly takes. The drawing ships. The assembly reveals what the drawing got wrong. The next version is better because someone assembled it.

Do not wait for the perfect manual. The manual does not exist. What exists is the parts in front of you, the drawing that is approximately correct, the hex torque you named slightly wrong but used exactly right, and the next turn visible from where you are standing.

Do not buy books too snobby to fit the Billy. The book that requires bespoke shelving to store is the book that lives in a pile on the floor. The framework that needs three days of setup before hello world. The specification so complete it takes longer to read than to implement. All in a pile while the flat-pack waits.

The Billy has been 202cm tall and 40cm deep since 1979. Every book that fits on a Billy fits on every Billy everywhere. The standard is the infrastructure. Conform to it and your knowledge travels. Design past it and it needs bespoke shelving.

## Don't Panic

Adams put "Don't Panic" on the cover of the Guide because the universe is large, confusing, and mostly indifferent to whether you understand it. The correct response is to pick up your towel and find the next turn. Not to sit down and calculate the optimal response to being lost in infinite space.

The Ikea instructions are the universe. The hex torque is the towel. The closet standing up is the next turn found.

Penny did not panic. The closet stands. The clothes have somewhere to go. The guys are still debating the reinforcement strategy.

Penny recognised Woz. Trillian packed for the apocalypse. The editor catches the obvious mistakes. All three are the weak link willing to learn, in different registers. All three know where the towel is. None of them needed the instructions to tell them.

The 90% signal is always in the room. You just have to be looking at the person, not at the domain.
