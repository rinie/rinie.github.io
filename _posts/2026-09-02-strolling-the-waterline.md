[Moving along the waterline](https://rinie.github.io/2026/08/31/moving-along-the-waterline/) was the mechanic. [Walking it](https://rinie.github.io/2026/09/01/drown-desert-or-move-along/) was the discipline — go back, check, don't let the water recede without noticing. Strolling is neither of those, and it's the one that actually describes what happened here.

Nobody planned 100+ posts about the boundary between the physical and the meaningful. There was no outline that said the eleventh post would be about a coffee machine that doesn't remember your order, and the hundred-and-first would be about why hard drives spent fifty years at 512 bytes and doubled to 4096. Nothing connected a phone number surviving a carrier switch to malloc's two function names to a rural German district digitising its jobcenter before touching anything a citizen would see — except that all three sat on the same waterline, looked at from wherever the last post happened to leave off standing.

That's what strolling actually is, as distinct from the other two verbs. Walking has a job: go check, come back, report. Strolling has no job at all. You look at the water because looking at the water is interesting, and you keep looking, for no better reason than that the last thing you saw made you want to see what was a little further along. Nobody strolls to a schedule. Nobody strolls with a destination that would make stopping feel like failure.

This wasn't a testament to planning. It's what a hundred small unplanned observations look like once you stop expecting each one to justify itself against a master outline. Wozniak not being recognised by physicists. An IKEA catalogue's actual packaging philosophy. A latency table from 2012 that aged unevenly on purpose. None of these were on a roadmap. Each one was just the next stretch of water, visible from wherever the last post happened to leave off.

Worse ages better than perfect had a companion nobody named until now: a stroll ages better than a march. A march has to justify every step against the plan. A stroll only has to stay near the water — and the water, it turns out, runs past an enormous number of interesting things, provided nobody's in a hurry to get anywhere in particular.
